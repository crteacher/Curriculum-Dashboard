Derivatives of Inverse Functions

Calculus I  ·  §3.3.5  ·  Christopher Halverson  ·  Fall 2026  ·  STUDENT

You know that $e^x$ and $\ln x$ are inverses — mirror images across the line $y=x$. If you already know the slope of $e^x$ at a point, can you find the slope of $\ln x$ at the mirrored point — without differentiating $\ln x$ from scratch?
The Inverse Function Derivative Rule

If $f$ is differentiable and invertible near a point, with inverse $g=f^{-1}$, then

$$g'(x)=\dfrac{1}{f'(g(x))}\qquad\text{provided }f'(g(x))\neq0$$

In words: the derivative of the inverse, at $x$, is the reciprocal of the derivative of the original function, evaluated at $g(x)$ — not at $x$ itself.

Deriving It With the Chain Rule

Since $f(g(x))=x$ for all $x$ in the domain of $g$, differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[f(g(x))\big]=\dfrac{d}{dx}[x]$$ $$f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)=1\qquad(\text{chain rule})$$

Solve for $g'(x)$: divide both sides by $f'(g(x))$.

▸ Why the Slopes Are Reciprocals (Geometric View)

Reflecting a graph across the line $y=x$ swaps every point $(a,b)$ with $(b,a)$ — and swaps the roles of "rise" and "run" at that point.

If $f$ has slope $m$ at $(a,b)$ (rise $m$ per unit run), then after reflecting, the same tangent line has slope $\dfrac{1}{m}$ at $(b,a)$ — rise and run have traded places.

That reflected tangent line is exactly the tangent to $g=f^{-1}$ at $x=b$. Since $g(b)=a$, this says $g'(b)=\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}=\dfrac{1}{f'(g(b))}$ — matching the formula derived algebraically at left.

□
▸ Common Mistake

Students compute $f'(x)$ and then plug in $x$ directly, instead of first finding $g(x)=f^{-1}(x)$ and evaluating $f'$ there.

The formula needs $f'(g(x))$, not $f'(x)$. Always find $g(x)$ first — often by solving $f(\text{something})=x$ — then substitute that something into $f'$.

Worked Examples
Example 1  —  Deriving $\ln'(x)=1/x$
Use $f(x)=e^x$ (whose inverse is $g(x)=\ln x$) and the inverse derivative rule to find $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x]$.
$f'(x)=e^x$, so $f'(g(x))=e^{\ln x}=x$.
$g'(x)=\dfrac{1}{f'(g(x))}=\dfrac{1}{x}$
This is exactly the familiar rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x]=\dfrac{1}{x}$ — now justified.
Example 2  —  Evaluating $(f^{-1})'$ at a point
Let $f(x)=x^3+2x+1$. Find $(f^{-1})'(4)$.
Find $g(4)$: solve $f(a)=4$. Try $a=1$: $1+2+1=4$ ✓. So $g(4)=1$.
$f'(x)=3x^2+2$, so $f'(1)=3+2=5$.
$(f^{-1})'(4)=\dfrac{1}{f'(1)}=\dfrac{1}{5}$
Example 3  —  Deriving $\arcsin'(x)$
Use $f(x)=\sin x$ on $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ (inverse $g(x)=\arcsin x$) to derive $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\arcsin x]$.
$f'(x)=\cos x$, so $f'(g(x))=\cos(\arcsin x)$.
If $\theta=\arcsin x$, then $\sin\theta=x$, and $\cos\theta=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ (right triangle: opposite $x$, hypotenuse 1).
$g'(x)=\dfrac{1}{\cos(\arcsin x)}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$
Example 4  —  Deriving $\arctan'(x)$
Use $f(x)=\tan x$ (inverse $g(x)=\arctan x$) to derive $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\arctan x]$.
$f'(x)=\sec^2x=1+\tan^2x$, so $f'(g(x))=1+\tan^2(\arctan x)=1+x^2$.
$g'(x)=\dfrac{1}{f'(g(x))}=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$
Reference — The Six Inverse Trig Derivatives
$\dfrac{d}{dx}[\arcsin x]=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$$\dfrac{d}{dx}[\text{arccsc }x]=-\dfrac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^2-1}}$
$\dfrac{d}{dx}[\arccos x]=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$$\dfrac{d}{dx}[\text{arcsec }x]=\dfrac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^2-1}}$
$\dfrac{d}{dx}[\arctan x]=\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$$\dfrac{d}{dx}[\text{arccot }x]=-\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$
Practice Problems
1.
Use $f(x)=e^x$ to verify $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\ln x]=\dfrac{1}{x}$ at the specific point $x=e^2$.
$g(e^2)=\ln(e^2)=2$. $f'(2)=e^2$. $g'(e^2)=\dfrac{1}{e^2}$ — matches $\dfrac{1}{x}$ at $x=e^2$.
2.
Let $f(x)=x^3$. Find $(f^{-1})'(8)$.
$g(8)=2$ (since $2^3=8$). $f'(2)=12$. $(f^{-1})'(8)=\dfrac{1}{12}$
3.
Let $f(x)=x^5+x$. Find $(f^{-1})'(2)$.
$g(2)=1$ (since $1+1=2$). $f'(x)=5x^4+1$, $f'(1)=6$. $(f^{-1})'(2)=\dfrac{1}{6}$
4.
Use $f(x)=\cos x$ (inverse $\arccos x$) to derive $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\arccos x]$.
$f'(x)=-\sin x$; $\sin(\arccos x)=\sqrt{1-x^2}$. $g'(x)=\dfrac{1}{-\sqrt{1-x^2}}=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$
5.
Let $f(x)=2x^3+x-4$. Find $(f^{-1})'(-1)$.
$g(-1)=1$ (since $2+1-4=-1$). $f'(x)=6x^2+1$, $f'(1)=7$. $(f^{-1})'(-1)=\dfrac{1}{7}$
6.
Explain why $(f^{-1})'(x)$ is undefined wherever $f'(g(x))=0$. Use $f(x)=x^3$ at $x=0$ as an example.
The formula divides by $f'(g(x))$ — division by zero is undefined. Geometrically, $f(x)=x^3$ has a horizontal tangent at $x=0$; reflecting a horizontal line across $y=x$ gives a vertical line, so $f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}$ has a vertical (undefined-slope) tangent at $x=0$.
