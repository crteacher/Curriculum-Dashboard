The Derivative as a Function

Calculus I  ·  §2.2.5  ·  Christopher Halverson  ·  Fall 2026  ·  INSTRUCTOR

We computed $f'(c)$ at specific points. But what if we leave $x$ as a variable? Then the derivative becomes a new function $f'(x)$ — one that tells us the slope at every point simultaneously. What does its graph reveal about $f$?
Derivative as a Function

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

Same definition — but $x$ is a variable, not a fixed number. The result is a new function whose output at any $x$ equals the slope of $f$ there.

Key results from the limit definition:
$f(x)=c \Rightarrow f'(x)=0$
$f(x)=ax+b \Rightarrow f'(x)=a$
$f(x)=ax^2 \Rightarrow f'(x)=2ax$

▸ Geometric Argument

The key idea: we want the slope of a curve at a single point $x$ — but a slope normally requires two points. Here's how the limit resolves that.

Take any nearby point $x+h$ on $f$. The slope of the secant line through $(x,\,f(x))$ and $(x+h,\,f(x+h))$ is the average rate of change: $$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

Now let $h\to 0$. The second point slides along the curve toward $x$. As it does, the secant line rotates and in the limit becomes the tangent line — the line that just grazes the curve at that point.

Capturing that limiting slope gives the derivative: $$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

This is the definition of the derivative, not a result derived from something else. Its justification is geometric: shrink the interval until the secant becomes the tangent, and you've captured the instantaneous rate of change.

∎

▸ Common Mistake

The classic error is expanding $f(x+h)$ as if $f$ distributes over the sum — writing $(x+h)^2 = x^2 + h^2$ and dropping the $2xh$ cross term. That cross term is the entire point: $(x+h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2$, so $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \frac{2xh+h^2}{h} = 2x+h \to 2x$. Drop the $2xh$ and the quotient collapses to $\frac{h^2}{h}=h\to 0$, "proving" every function has derivative $0$.

The other trap is plugging in $h=0$ at the start, getting $\frac{0}{0}$, and declaring the derivative undefined. You must substitute $x+h$ into the actual rule, simplify, and cancel the $h$ in the denominator first — only then let $h\to 0$.

Reading $f'$ from $f$

$f'(x)>0$ on interval → $f$ is increasing there
$f'(x)<0$ on interval → $f$ is decreasing there
$f'(x)=0$ at a point → $f$ has a horizontal tangent

The graph of $f'$:

  • Positive where $f$ goes uphill
  • Negative where $f$ goes downhill
  • Crosses zero where $f$ has peaks/valleys
▸ Common Mistake

Don't confuse the sign of $f'$ with the sign or value of $f$. $f'(x)=0$ means $f$ has a horizontal tangent there — it does not mean $f(x)=0$ (a root). A graph can sit far above the $x$-axis and still have $f'=0$ at a peak.

Likewise, $f'>0$ says $f$ is increasing, which has nothing to do with whether $f$ itself is positive: $f(x)=x-5$ has $f'(x)=1>0$ everywhere, so it rises across its whole domain even where its values are negative. Read $f'$ as slope, not height.

Notation: $f'(x)$, $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}[f(x)]$, and $Df(x)$ all mean the same thing.

$f(x)=x^2-4x$
$f'(x)=2x-4$

Where $f$ has its minimum ($x=2$, Example 4), $f'$ crosses zero — that's the horizontal tangent.

Worked Examples
Example 1  —  Derivative of $x^2$ as a function
Use the definition to find $f'(x)$ for $f(x)=x^2$.
$\dfrac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=\dfrac{2xh+h^2}{h}=2x+h\to 2x$
So $f'(x)=2x$.
Example 2  —  Derivative of $3x^2-x$
Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x)=3x^2-x$.
$\dfrac{3(x+h)^2-(x+h)-(3x^2-x)}{h}$
$=\dfrac{6xh+3h^2-h}{h}=6x+3h-1\to 6x-1$
Example 3  —  Reading increasing/decreasing
For $f(x)=x^2$, where is $f$ increasing? Decreasing?
$f'(x)=2x$
$f'(x)>0$ when $x>0$: increasing on $(0,\infty)$
$f'(x)<0$ when $x<0$: decreasing on $(-\infty,0)$
Example 4  —  Horizontal tangent
Find all $x$ where $f(x)=x^2-4x$ has a horizontal tangent.
$f'(x)=2x-4=0 \Rightarrow x=2$
Horizontal tangent at $x=2$.
Practice Problems
1.
Use the definition to find $f'(x)$ for $f(x)=5x^2$.
$\dfrac{5(x+h)^2-5x^2}{h}=10x+5h\to 10x$
2.
Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x)=2x^2+3x$.
$4x+3$
3.
If $f'(x)=3x^2-12$, where is $f$ increasing?
$3x^2-12>0 \Rightarrow x^2>4 \Rightarrow x<-2$ or $x>2$
4.
If $f'(x)=3x^2-12$, where does $f$ have a horizontal tangent?
$x=\pm 2$
5.
Interpret: $f'(5)=-3$ means what about $f$ near $x=5$?
$f$ is decreasing at $x=5$; tangent slope is $-3$
6.
Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{x}$ using the limit definition.
$\dfrac{\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac{1}{x}}{h}=\dfrac{-1}{x(x+h)}\to -\dfrac{1}{x^2}$
