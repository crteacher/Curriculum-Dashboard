FTC Part 2

Calculus I  ·  §4.4  ·  Christopher Halverson  ·  Fall 2026  ·  INSTRUCTOR

FTC Part 1 says $\dfrac{d}{dx}\!\left[\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\right]=f(x)$: differentiating an integral recovers $f$. What about the reverse? If you already know an antiderivative $F$ of $f$, can you evaluate $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ without summing a single rectangle?
FTC Part 2 & Its Proof

Theorem (FTC Part 2 / Newton–Leibniz):
Let $f$ be Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ and let $F$ be an antiderivative of $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$. Then

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)$$

Proof. Recall the MVT: if $F$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, there exists $c\in(a,b)$ with $F'(c)(b-a)=F(b)-F(a)$.

Partition $[a,b]$ with any points $a=x_0

(1) Insert additive inverses to telescope:

$F(b)\!-\!F(a)$$=[F(x_n)\!-\!F(x_{n-1})]+\cdots+[F(x_1)\!-\!F(x_0)]$$=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}[F(x_i)-F(x_{i-1})]$

(2) $F$ is continuous on $[x_{i-1},x_i]$ and differentiable on $(x_{i-1},x_i)$, so the MVT gives $c_i\in(x_{i-1},x_i)$ with

$F(x_i)-F(x_{i-1})=F'(c_i)\,\Delta x_i=f(c_i)\,\Delta x_i$

Substituting into (1):

$F(b)-F(a)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(c_i)\,\Delta x_i$

Take the limit as $\|\Delta x_i\|\to 0$ (largest partition width $\to 0$, $n\to\infty$):

$\displaystyle\lim_{\|\Delta x_i\|\to 0}F(b)-F(a)=\lim_{\|\Delta x_i\|\to 0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(c_i)\,\Delta x_i$

$F(b)-F(a)$ is constant (independent of the partition), so the left side is unchanged. The right side is the definition of the Riemann integral. Therefore:

$\displaystyle F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\qquad\square$

▸ Common Mistake

Evaluating at only the upper limit: $\int_1^3 2x\,dx\ne F(3)$. FTC Part 2 always requires $F(b)-F(a)$ — both endpoints.

Constants of integration cancel: $F(x)=x^2+7$ and $G(x)=x^2$ give $F(3)-F(1)=G(3)-G(1)=8$, so there is no need to find $C$ when evaluating a definite integral.

Net Change & Signed Area

Net Change Theorem. If $F'(x)$ is the rate of change of a quantity $F$, then

$$\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)$$

The integral of a rate gives the net change in the quantity.

Examples:

  • $v(t)=$ velocity $\Rightarrow$ $\displaystyle\int_a^b v(t)\,dt=$ net displacement
  • $P'(t)=$ population growth rate $\Rightarrow$ $\displaystyle\int_a^b P'\,dt=$ net population change
  • $C'(x)=$ marginal cost $\Rightarrow$ $\displaystyle\int_a^b C'\,dx=$ change in cost

Signed area. FTC Part 2 gives signed area: regions below the $x$-axis contribute negatively. Net displacement $\ne$ total distance when $v$ changes sign.

▸ Common Mistake

Confusing net displacement and total distance. If $v(t)$ is negative on part of $[a,b]$, $\int_a^b v\,dt$ gives net displacement (can be zero or negative), while total distance requires $\int_a^b |v(t)|\,dt$ — break the interval at the zeros of $v$ and add magnitudes.

FTC Part 2 is the bridge from §§4.1–4.3 to the rest of Calculus: instead of summing thousands of rectangles, you find one antiderivative and subtract. §4.5 builds the antiderivative toolkit.

Worked Examples
Example 1  —  FTC Part 2 — basic
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^2\,dx$.
Antiderivative: $F(x)=\dfrac{x^3}{3}$
$F(1)-F(0)=\dfrac{1}{3}-0=\dfrac{1}{3}$
Example 2  —  FTC Part 2 — polynomial
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^3(2x+1)\,dx$.
Antiderivative: $F(x)=x^2+x$
$F(3)-F(1)=(9+3)-(1+1)=12-2=10$
Example 3  —  Net displacement
A particle’s velocity is $v(t)=t^2-4$ on $[0,3]$. Find the net displacement.
Antiderivative: $F(t)=\dfrac{t^3}{3}-4t$
$F(3)-F(0)=(9-12)-0=-3$ units
Example 4  —  Verify with the limit
Confirm $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^2\,dx=\dfrac{1}{3}$ using $\lim_{n\to\infty}R_n$.
$\Delta x=1/n,\; x_i=i/n$
$R_n=\dfrac{1}{n^3}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^2=\dfrac{1}{n^3}\cdot\dfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}=\dfrac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}\to\dfrac{1}{3}$  ✓
Practice Problems
1.
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^2 x\,dx$ using FTC Part 2.
$F(x)=x^2/2$; $\;F(2)-F(0)=2$
2.
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^4 3x^2\,dx$.
$F(x)=x^3$; $\;F(4)-F(1)=64-1=63$
3.
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^3(x^2+1)\,dx$.
$F(x)=x^3/3+x$; $\;F(3)-F(0)=12$
4.
A particle has velocity $v(t)=2t-1$ on $[0,4]$. Net displacement?
$F(t)=t^2-t$; $\;F(4)-F(0)=12$ units
5.
In one sentence: why does $F(b)-F(a)=\displaystyle\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx$?
MVT gives $F(b)-F(a)=\sum f(c_i)\Delta x$ for any partition, and that Riemann sum converges to $\int_a^b F'\,dx$ as $n\to\infty$.
6.
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_2^2 \sin(x^3)\,dx$.
$0$ — same upper and lower limit (integral property from §4.3).
