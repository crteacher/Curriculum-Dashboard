Wrap-up & Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem

Voting Theory  ·  Part 6.9  ·  Christopher Halverson  ·  Fall 2026  ·  STUDENT

We have collected four fairness criteria. Do you think some voting method could satisfy all of them, every time?
Recall the Criteria

Fairness criteria are statements that seem like they should be true in a fair election.

Condorcet Criterion: if one choice is preferred in every one-to-one comparison with the other choices, that choice (the Condorcet winner) should win the election.

Monotonicity Criterion: if voters change their ballots to increase the preference for a candidate, it should not harm that candidate’s chances of winning.

Majority Criterion: if a choice has a majority (more than everyone else put together) of the first-place votes, that choice should win.

Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA): if a non-winning choice is added to or removed from the ballot, it should not change the winner.

Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem

No. Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem says that with three or more choices, no ranked voting method can satisfy every reasonable fairness criterion at once.

Every method we studied breaks at least one criterion in some election (see the scorecard below). Choosing a voting method means choosing which flaws to live with.

Discussion Questions
Practice Problems
1.
Which method violates the fewest criteria in our table? Is it automatically the best method?
Copeland violates only IIA in our table. But “fewest violations” is not the same as “best”: criteria matter differently in different contexts.
2.
Plurality is the most common method in the US. Which criteria does it violate, and who is hurt?
Plurality can violate Condorcet and IIA; it encourages insincere voting and splits similar candidates.
3.
If no method is perfect, how should a community decide which to use?
Open discussion: decide which criteria matter most (e.g., majority rule vs. protecting compromise candidates) and which flaws are least harmful.
4.
Pick one method and one criterion it violates. Build a small election that shows the violation.
Examples: Borda & Majority (Part 3), IRV & Condorcet (Part 2.5), Copeland & IIA (Part 4).
