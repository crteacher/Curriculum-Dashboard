Apportionment & Hamilton’s Method

Voting Theory  ·  Part 7  ·  Christopher Halverson  ·  Fall 2026  ·  INSTRUCTOR

Pizzas, seats in Congress, ambulances for hospitals: you have to split a fixed number of whole things among groups of different sizes. How do you do it fairly?
Apportionment

Apportionment means dividing something up (pizzas, ambulances, seats for states) using these rules:

  • The things being divided exist only in whole numbers.
  • We use all of them, and no more.
  • Each group gets at least one.
  • Each group’s share is approximately proportional to its population.
Hamilton’s Method

1. Standard divisor = total population ÷ seats.

2. Quota for each group = its population ÷ standard divisor.

3. Give each group its quota rounded down (lower quota).

4. Hand out the leftover seats, one each, to the groups with the largest fractional parts.

Worked Examples
Example 1  —  Seats for cities
5 seats among City A (10 people), City B (7), City C (3).
Standard divisor = 20 ÷ 5 = 4. Quota = pop ÷ divisor.

City A102.50020.5002
City B71.75010.7502
City C30.75000.7501
Lower quotas use 3 of 5 seats; 2 left over go to the largest fractions.
Example 2  —  Pizza order (tie!)
5 pizzas for 20 people: 11 want pepperoni, 7 cheese, 2 sausage.
Standard divisor = 20 ÷ 5 = 4. Quota = pop ÷ divisor.
GroupPop.QuotaLowerFractionFinal
Pepperoni112.75020.7503
Cheese71.75010.7502
Sausage20.50000.5000
Lower quotas use 3 of 5 seats; 2 left over go to the largest fractions.
Pepperoni and cheese tie at .75, and only 2 seats remain: both receive one. Sausage gets none, which breaks the “at least one” rule, a known drawback of Hamilton for small groups.
Example 3  —  States and electors

Example 4  —  The Alabama paradox
Populations A: 5, B: 5, C: 3 (thousand). Apportion 6 seats, then 7 seats.
6 seats: A 2, B 2, C 2.
7 seats: A 3, B 3, C 1.
Adding a seat made C lose a seat: the Alabama paradox.
