Borda Count & the Majority Criterion

Voting Theory  ·  Part 3  ·  Christopher Halverson  ·  Fall 2026  ·  STUDENT

Instead of only counting first places, what if every ranking position earned points? What would happen to a candidate who is everyone’s second choice?
Borda Count

Borda Count: with $k$ choices, a ballot gives $k$ points to its 1st choice, $k-1$ to its 2nd, …, and $1$ point to its last. Total the points over all ballots; the largest total wins.

Unranked candidates: if a ballot leaves choices blank, a common fix is to give each unranked choice an equal share of the remaining points.

Majority Criterion

Majority Criterion: if a choice has a majority (more than everyone else put together) of the first-place votes, that choice should win.

Which of our methods can violate it? Plurality and IRV never can (a majority winner always takes the lead). Borda can — see Example 2.

Worked Examples

Example 2  —  Borda violates Majority
Votes843
1st choiceACB
2nd choiceBBC
3rd choiceCDD
4th choiceDAA
4 points for 1st down to 1 for last. Who wins? Does anyone have a majority of first-place votes?
A: 8·4 + 4·1 + 3·1 = 39
B: 8·3 + 4·3 + 3·4 = 48
C: 8·2 + 4·4 + 3·3 = 41
D: 8·1 + 4·2 + 3·2 = 22
Borda winner: B. But A has 8 of 15 first-place votes (a majority), so Borda violates the Majority Criterion.
Example 3  —  What if voters dislike A and C?

The 14 voters rank B first but C last. Rerun Borda.
A: 15·3 + 14·2 + 7·1 = 80
B: 15·2 + 14·3 + 7·2 = 86
C: 15·1 + 14·1 + 7·3 = 50
Winner: B.
Example 4  —  Check Condorcet

Is the Borda winner also the Condorcet winner?
A vs B: 22–14 (A wins)
A vs C: 29–7 (A wins)
B vs C: 29–7 (B wins)
Condorcet winner: A; Borda winner: A.
