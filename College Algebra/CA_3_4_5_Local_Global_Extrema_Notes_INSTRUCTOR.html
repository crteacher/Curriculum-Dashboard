Local and Global Extrema

College Algebra  ·  §3.4.5  ·  Christopher Halverson  ·  Fall 2026  ·  INSTRUCTOR

On $[0,7]$ a graph has peaks at $x=2$ (height 4.2) and $x=5.5$ (height 3.0), and it starts at height 4.8. Where is the global maximum — at one of the peaks, or somewhere else? What should you always check besides the interior peaks?
Local (Relative) Extrema

A local maximum is a point where the function value is higher than all nearby points — the function switches from increasing to decreasing.

A local minimum is a point where the function value is lower than all nearby points — the function switches from decreasing to increasing.

Think of them as peaks (local max) and valleys (local min) of the graph.

  • We say “local max/min at $x=c$” (the x-value) or “local max/min value of $f(c)$” (the y-value)
  • A function can have multiple local maxima and minima
► Common Mistake

Students often say "the local max is 3" when they mean the local max occurs at $x=3$, or that the local max value is some $y$-number. Be precise: there are two separate pieces of information — the $x$-value where the extremum occurs, and the $f(x)$ value there.

Also: a local max does not have to be the highest point on the whole graph. It just has to be higher than the points immediately around it.

Global (Absolute) Extrema

The global maximum is the single highest output value over the entire domain or interval — the tallest point on the whole graph.

The global minimum is the single lowest output value.

On a closed interval $[a, b]$: global extrema occur at one of:

  • A local maximum or minimum in the interior, or
  • An endpoint of the interval

Always check the endpoints — the global max/min may not be at a peak or valley.

Reading the Graph

On $[0,7]$: two local maxima (at $x=2$ and $x=5.5$) and one local minimum (at $x=4$). The global max is at the left endpoint $x=0$ — even though $x=2$ looks like a peak, the starting value is higher. The global min is at the right endpoint $x=7$ — lower than the local min at $x=4$.

Worked Examples
Example 1 — Reading local extrema
A function increases on $(-\infty, 3)$, decreases on $(3, 7)$, and increases on $(7, \infty)$. Identify any local extrema.
Local max at $x=3$ (switches ↑↓). Local min at $x=7$ (switches ↓↑). No global max/min on all of $\mathbb{R}$.
Example 2 — Global extrema can be at endpoints
On $[0, 6]$, a function has a local max value of $5$ at $x=2$, and $f(0)=7$, $f(6)=1$. What is the global maximum value?
Check all candidates: local max $=5$, left endpoint $f(0)=7$, right endpoint $f(6)=1$. Global max $=\mathbf{7}$ at $x=0$. The endpoint beats the interior peak.
Example 3 — No local extrema
$f(x)=3x-2$ on $[-1, 4]$. Does this function have any local maxima or minima? What about global?
A line never changes direction — no local extrema. Global max at right endpoint: $f(4)=10$. Global min at left endpoint: $f(-1)=-5$.
Example 4 — Value vs. location
A function has a local min at $x=4$ with $f(4)=2$. State: (a) where the local min is, (b) what the local min value is.
(a) The local min occurs at $x=4$ — this is an $x$-value (location).
(b) The local min value is $f(4)=2$ — this is a $y$-value (height).
Practice Problems
1.
A function decreases on $(-\infty,5)$ and increases on $(5,\infty)$. Is $x=5$ a local max or local min?
Local min (function switches from decreasing to increasing)
2.
On $[-2, 8]$, $f$ has a local max value of $10$ at $x=3$, and $f(-2)=12$, $f(8)=4$. Find the global max and global min values.
Global max $=12$ at endpoint $x=-2$; global min $=4$ at endpoint $x=8$
3.
Describe the difference between “local max at $x=2$” and “local max value of $6$.”
$x=2$ is the location (input); $6=f(2)$ is the value (output/height)
4.
Can a global max also be a local max? Give an example.
Yes — e.g., $f(x)=-(x-3)^2$ has one peak at $x=3$, which is both a local and global max on $(-\infty,\infty)$.
5.
$f(x) = -(x-2)^2 + 9$ on $[0,5]$. The vertex is at $(2,9)$. Evaluate at the endpoints and state the global max and global min.
$f(0)=5$, $f(2)=9$ (global max), $f(5)=0$ (global min)
6.
How many local maxima can a function have on its entire domain?
Any number (zero, one, two, ...) — a function can have multiple local maxima
